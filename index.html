INFRASTRUCTURAL FRICTION

Three lots.
One job.

Raise a signal lantern so it carries west to the valley. Working out how high, and where, is the same problem every time.

What changes from lot to lot is none of that. It is the pick in your hand, how far the stone sits, whether there is light, and what stands between you and the material.

 WHAT THE THREE LOTS COST

Same builder.
Same judgment.

The thinking did not change across the three lots. Where the lantern had to go was worked out fresh each time, and the working out was free every time. What changed was whether you could get to it, and what it cost you on the way.

Difficulty that makes the work worth doing is productive. A wall you cannot get through is not difficulty; it is exclusion. Telling them apart is the whole skill, and it is a question about conditions rather than about people.

 THE CONDITIONING BASE

It did not get easier.
It got possible.

Infrastructural friction is the material and systemic layer underneath the learning: time, access, tools, policy, the room itself. It is not a fourth kind of difficulty sitting alongside the thinking, the articulating, and the becoming. It is what decides whether a learner ever reaches any of them.

Pedagogical friction, and this account of its infrastructural base, are Micah Miner's proposed contributions. This is a teaching model, not a measurement of anyone's learning, and it reports no research findings.

What this is showing

Three lots, one job, one builder. The judgment the job asks for is identical on all three and cannot be made easier by any change to the conditions. What differs is the tool, the distance, the light, and whether anything stands between you and the material.

The point is the difference between difficulty that is worth keeping and a barrier that only looks like difficulty. Awkward controls are neither; they are a defect, and if something here is fighting you, that is a bug rather than a lesson.
